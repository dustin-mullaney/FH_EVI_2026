# Weeks 4-6: Target selection and project planning

Now we pick what we actually build. Spend the first week thinking broadly — don't
lock onto your first idea. By week 6, bring me 2-3 candidate targets.

A "target" here means a specific design problem: a molecule you want to bind, an
activity you want to create, a structure you want to build.

## Where to look for ideas

The Baker review makes a claim worth taking seriously: for binders, the hard part
is no longer *how* to design, but *what* to design to and for what application.
They say the same thing about multivalent receptor agonists — the open question is
which pairs or triplets of receptors are worth bringing together at all. That is a
biology question, and it's the kind of question you are allowed to answer.

Some categories from the review, as prompts rather than a menu:

- **Therapeutic binders.** Over 200 validated protein targets so far (their
  Supplementary Table 1 — worth skimming to see what's taken). Antivirals, snake
  venom and *C. difficile* toxin neutralizers, receptor antagonists and agonists,
  binders to peptide-MHC complexes for cancer neo-epitopes.
- **Previously "undruggable" targets.** Intrinsically disordered regions and loose
  peptides, now reachable by induced-fit design — β-catenin, amyloid-β and tau
  traps.
- **Trafficking.** EndoTags and blood-brain-barrier receptor binders: proteins
  whose function is to *move* a cargo or *destroy* a target rather than just block
  it.
- **Small-molecule binders and sensors.** Therapeutic sponges, drug-level monitors,
  the fentanyl sensor put into plants.
- **Nanopores and materials.** Custom pore sizes and linings for protein
  sequencing; 2D arrays, fibers, designed crystals.
- **Sustainability.** CaCO₃ templating for CO₂ fixation, ZnO nucleation for
  semiconductor biosynthesis, chlorophyll special pairs.
- **Genome regulation.** Compact de novo DNA binders as an alternative to zinc
  fingers, TALEs and Cas.
- **Hard stuff.** Enzymes for high-barrier reactions, switches, nanomachines. They
  list these as unsolved. That's a reason to be interested, not a reason to stay
  away — but know what you're walking into.

You can also pick a target the review doesn't mention at all. That's often the more
interesting move.

## For each candidate, work out

1. What is the protein supposed to do? State it concretely enough that someone
   else could tell whether it worked.

2. Has anyone tried this, or something close? If nobody has, spend time figuring
   out why not. Sometimes it's because the idea is new, and sometimes it's because
   of a problem you haven't spotted yet.

3. Which approach fits — structure-based design, a sequence model, or both? Say
   why, using what you read in weeks 1-3. If your target is a flexible peptide or
   an IDR, that choice matters a lot.

4. What do you need before you can start? A structure of the target? A binding
   site you can actually point at? Known binders to learn from? A big sequence
   family?

5. How would you know it worked? Describe a first experiment, not a finished
   product. "It cures the disease" is not a measurement. Note that the review flags
   assay development as a real bottleneck for anything past binding and catalysis —
   if you can't name the readout, that is part of your answer.

6. If it works, who cares and why? The review's argument for snake venom binders
   is partly that designed proteins are thermostable and don't need a cold chain.
   That kind of reasoning — why *this* modality for *this* problem — is what I'm
   looking for.

You will not be able to answer all of these for all three targets, and that's
useful information. A question you can't answer yet is often the real reason a
target is hard.
